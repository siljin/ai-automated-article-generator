# Lesson and Case Structure

## The Core Inversion: Concept First, Cases as Evidence

Both cases exist to demonstrate the concept, not the other way around. Teach the concept and its framework in full before naming any company. State it as something the reader will use, not something they will read about.

## Learn Module — Required Sections (in order)

1. **Simple explanation.** Open with an everyday analogy before any PM terminology. Then answer directly: what is this concept, why does it matter, what problem does it solve for a PM, when should a PM use it, and — just as important — when should a PM *not* use it (this last part is usually skipped and is where average answers get exposed).
2. **Interview relevance.** Why interviewers test this concept; what a strong candidate demonstrates vs what an average candidate misses; what interviewers are actually listening for; 2-3 example interview prompts where this concept would be the right tool.
3. **Mental model / framework.** 3-7 steps. For every step: the question the PM should ask, the information required, the decision being made, the common mistake at that step, and what a strong answer sounds like. Never present the framework as a bare list to memorize — the "why" for each step must be explicit.
4. **Visual explanation.** Pick whichever form fits the concept: 2x2 matrix, funnel, decision tree, hierarchy/tree, metric tree, prioritization matrix, or before/after workflow. The visual must make the reasoning easier to follow, not just decorate the page.
5. **Numerical example.** A worked, concrete example with realistic (but generic — not tied to the Observe module's real company) numbers: segment sizes, conversion rates, RICE scores, funnel drop-offs, whatever fits. Show every calculation, not just the final number.
6. **Common failure modes.** At least five, each with a concrete correction — e.g. jumping to solutions before naming the user, treating all users/options as equally important, picking a vanity metric, applying a framework mechanically without judgment, ignoring strategic fit, failing to state assumptions, optimizing one metric while damaging another, recommending an action without defining what success looks like.
7. **Cheat sheet.** Compact: core one-line definition, the framework steps, three questions to remember, three common traps, one example sentence a strong candidate could actually say out loud.

## Observe Module — Required Structure

1. **Company and product context.** The company, the relevant product, primary users, business model, product strategy, competitive environment, and the current market/product dynamic relevant to the concept. Every specific number here is tagged FACT, ESTIMATE, or ASSUMPTION per sourcing-and-citations.md.
2. **The product problem.** One concrete problem a real PM there could plausibly own (not "improve the product" — a specific, scoped decision). State: the user problem, the business problem, why it matters *now*, constraints, what's known, and what's genuinely unknown (flag anything the case needs that isn't disclosed as an ASSUMPTION here).
3. **PM application walkthrough.** Walk the Learn module's framework step by step against this real problem. For every step show: the question, the available evidence (tagged), the assumptions made, the analysis, the decision at that step, the trade-off, and the risk. Do not skip straight to the answer — the reasoning must be visible and explicit, the same way a strong candidate would narrate it live.
4. **Decision and recommendation.** Recommended action, target user, product change, expected user impact, expected business impact, success metrics, guardrail metrics, main risks, and what should be tested before scaling further.
5. **Transferable lesson.** Explicitly warn the reader not to copy this company's specific answer as "the framework." Restate the general, reusable move in a way that would transfer to a different company and industry.

## Fact/Inference/Assumption Visibility

The Observe module must make it visually easy to tell these three apart at a glance (e.g. small distinct tags/badges rendered next to the claim): a **verified fact** (cited), a **reasonable inference** (follows from facts but isn't itself stated anywhere), and a **hypothetical assumption made for teaching** (the company doesn't disclose this; labeled explicitly). Never blend an assumption into the narrative so it reads like a reported number.

## What NOT to Do

- Do not open the Observe module with the company name before the concept has been taught — concept first, always.
- Do not let the Observe module's specific numbers stand in for the Learn module's numerical example — keep the generic teaching example and the real-company case numbers clearly separate, since the teaching example needs to be simple and the real case needs to be accurate.
- Do not present the company's actual real-world decision as the one objectively correct answer — the Transferable Lesson section exists specifically to prevent that takeaway.
