# Article Structure

## Central Paradox

Every strong research article opens with a tension: something surprising, counterintuitive, or inconsistent with the conventional story. The first 1 to 2 sentences must state this paradox directly.

Good paradox patterns:

- `X is the world's [superlative], yet its share of [metric] is only [small number].`
- `Despite [favorable condition A], [outcome B] has not materialized.`
- `[Country/company/sector] grew at [rate], but through [unusual channel] rather than the conventional path.`

## Research Questions

Pose exactly 2 to 3 explicit research questions in the introduction. These are not rhetorical; each question maps to a major section.

Required format: `This note addresses [two/three] questions: First, [question one]? Second, [question two]? Third, [question three]?` Omit the third clause only when the article has two research questions.

## Required Section Sequence

### Section 1: Introduction

- Paragraph 1: The paradox. One to two sentences maximum. No preamble.
- Paragraph 2: Context and scale. Name key metrics and compare to the most relevant benchmark peer or reference case.
- Paragraph 3: The divergence. Explain how the subject differs from conventional theory or historical precedent.
- Paragraph 4: Research questions. State 2 to 3 explicit questions.

### Section 2: Background - Trajectory and Structural Context

Subsection 2A: Trajectory and Baseline Conditions

- Historical trajectory over at least a decade when available, or the longest credible period if the topic is new or event-based.
- Comparison to 3 to 5 benchmark peers, prior cycles, regions, firms, products, institutions, or reference cases, depending on the topic.
- Current level on the key metric relative to the most relevant benchmark.
- Baseline tailwinds and headwinds: demographic, institutional, technological, fiscal, regulatory, market, operational, or geographic as appropriate.

Subsection 2B: Structural Transformation

- Structural change, business-model mechanics, policy design, market structure, or institutional incentives.
- Comparison to the canonical path, prior cycle, peer benchmark, or counterfactual most relevant to the topic.
- Stacked bar, line chart, or equivalent visualization showing shares over time.
- The specific structural gap that creates the central tension.

### Section 3: Research Question One

- Restate the question as the section's thesis challenge.
- Name and quantify the obstacle.
- Present evidence for the thesis.
- Present evidence against or limits to the thesis.
- Explain the policy, institutional, or market context.
- Insert 1 to 2 charts at data-dense points.
- End with an honest section-level conclusion.

### Section 4: Research Question Two

Use the same structure as Section 3. Do not truncate unless the evidence genuinely warrants a shorter section.

### Section 5: Research Question Three, If Applicable

Only include if a third question was posed and merits a full section. Use the same structure as Sections 3 and 4.

### Section 6: Conclusion

Do not summarize. Synthesize implications for external actors.

- Paragraph 1: Restate the central challenge in one sentence, then pivot to the most likely trajectory under partial success.
- Paragraph 2: Economic implications for external actors.
- Paragraph 3: Geopolitical, institutional, technological, or structural implications.
- Final sentence: The most important unresolved question.

## Writing Standards

- Every sentence must advance the argument.
- Never write "significantly" without a number.
- Benchmark every major claim against at least one peer.
- Every statistic carries an inline citation.
- Label each figure's provenance per the data tiers (FACT, ESTIMATE, ILLUSTRATION); see sourcing-and-citations.md.
- Distinguish observation (what the data shows), insight (why it is non-obvious or matters), and implication (what a decision-maker should do); analytical prose and every chart interpretation must reach at least the insight level, never stop at restating the data.
- Hedge only when uncertainty is material.
- Introduction: 4 to 6 paragraphs.
- Background: 4 to 6 paragraphs with at least 2 charts.
- Each research-question section: 5 to 8 paragraphs with 1 to 2 charts.
- Conclusion: 3 to 4 paragraphs.
- No bullet points in analytical prose inside the final article.

## Plain Language

Write so a smart reader who is new to the topic can follow every sentence on the first read. Keep the analysis deep, but make the words simple. Simpler language, not simpler thinking.

- Prefer short sentences. Aim for one idea per sentence; if a sentence runs past about 25 words or needs two reads, split it.
- Choose the plain word over the fancy one: "use" not "utilize", "shows" not "demonstrates", "so" or "because" not "thereby" or "owing to the fact that", "enough" not "sufficient", "start" not "commence", "about" not "approximately".
- Cut filler openers: "it is important to note that", "it is worth highlighting", "in order to", "the fact that". Say the thing directly.
- Avoid long noun stacks and abstraction pile-ups ("revenue optimization capability enablement"). Name the actor and the action: who does what, and what happens.
- Go easy on dashes and semicolons. If a sentence leans on several, rewrite it as two plain sentences.
- Spell out and explain every technical term, acronym, or piece of jargon the first time it appears, in everyday words. After that you may reuse it.
- Read each paragraph aloud in your head. If you lose the thread or run out of breath, simplify.

This standard does not lower the rigor of the argument, the citations, or the quantitative reasoning. It only changes the diction and sentence shape.

## Per-Page Glossary

Every page (section) of the article ends with a short **Glossary** of the new terms and acronyms introduced on that page. The glossary is how the reader keeps up without prior expertise.

- List only terms that are new on this page. Do not repeat a term already defined on an earlier page.
- Each entry is the term (acronyms spelled out in full) followed by a one-line, plain-language definition a newcomer can understand. Avoid defining a term with other jargon.
- Keep definitions to a single sentence. The goal is recognition, not a textbook entry.
- If a page introduces no new term or acronym, omit the glossary for that page rather than padding it.
- Glossary definitions are reference aids; never write a scored question whose answer is simply a glossary definition.
