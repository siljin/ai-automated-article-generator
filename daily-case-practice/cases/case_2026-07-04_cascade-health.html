Cascade Health Partners — should we roll out ambient AI scribes system-wide?

Investment Decision / Cost-Benefit AI + Healthcare Difficulty: Easy-Medium (short drill) ~15 min

Prompt

Your client is Cascade Health Partners, a 14-hospital nonprofit health system in the Pacific Northwest with about 900 physicians and advanced practice providers on staff.

A six-month pilot of an ambient AI documentation tool (a "digital scribe" that listens to visits and drafts clinical notes) at two hospitals just wrapped up. Clinicians who used it regularly liked it, but usage was inconsistent, and the measured time savings were smaller than the CFO expected going in.

The CFO wants to know: should Cascade sign a system-wide, multi-year contract with the vendor, and if so, on what terms?

  • The pilot tool integrates with Cascade's existing EHR (Epic).
  • Cascade's operating margin has been thin the last two years (~1.5%), so the CFO is wary of new fixed costs.
  • The vendor is one of a handful of enterprise-grade ambient AI players already used by peer health systems.
  • Clinician burnout and turnover are a standing board-level concern independent of this decision.

Step 3 — Brainstorm

Q: Beyond documentation time savings, what other ways could a system-wide ambient AI rollout create value or risk for Cascade — and how would you validate each before signing a multi-year contract?


Revenue-side: more complete capture of billing complexity (notes that better reflect the actual visit tend to support higher-acuity coding); fewer late/incomplete notes that delay claims submission; potential to see slightly more patients per day if documentation time is genuinely freed up rather than just shifted.

Cost-side: reduced reliance on human medical scribes or transcription services where those are still used; lower overtime/after-hours "pajama time" charting; possible reduction in physician turnover cost if burnout improves.

Risk-side: a "coding arms race" dynamic where payers get suspicious of systematically higher coding levels and tighten audits; AI transcription errors that go uncaught because clinicians trust the draft too much; multi-year vendor lock-in just as a wave of new, cheaper entrants is hitting the market.

Sanity check: that breakeven only holds if all 900 clinicians actually use the tool consistently — but the pilot showed just ~65% real adoption. If the billing uplift only accrues to clinicians who actually use the tool regularly, the realistic benefit is closer to 900 × 65% × $3,000 ≈ $1.76M against a full $2.70M cost — a roughly $0.94M net loss in year one on the CFO's own numbers, before counting integration and training costs.

That reframes the real question: the deal only pencils out if Cascade can either (a) negotiate pricing tied to actual usage/seats activated rather than headcount enrolled, or (b) invest in adoption (workflow redesign, champions, EHR tuning) to close the gap between enrollment and real usage — which is itself a cost and execution risk, not a given.

Step 5 — Quick take

In two or three sentences, what would you tell the CFO — go, no-go, or go with conditions — and why?


Model take: Don't sign the full system-wide, multi-year deal on the vendor's headcount-based pricing — the pilot's own adoption data undercuts the breakeven case. Instead, negotiate a shorter initial term with usage-based or usage-tiered pricing, expand first to the hospitals/departments that showed the highest pilot engagement, and tie any further expansion to hitting a real (not enrolled) usage threshold plus independent verification that the billing uplift isn't just aggressive upcoding that invites payer audits.

Biggest risk: locking into a multi-year, headcount-priced contract based on vendor-claimed benchmarks, then discovering — as Cascade's own pilot already hinted — that usage plateaus well below the level needed to justify the cost, with no easy exit.
